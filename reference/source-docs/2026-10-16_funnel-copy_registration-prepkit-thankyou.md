## Section 2: The Problem

### Section headline

## New Promise. Same Pattern. Another Disappointment.

### Body copy

The partner says they'll make more effort. The coworker says you won't have to cover for them again. The friend says they'll be there next time.

So you make room. Give another chance. Expect something different.

And when the same thing happens, you're left wondering whether to explain it again, try harder, or walk away.

People can grow. But there is a difference between leaving room for growth and making today's decisions as if that growth has already happened.

Dr. Daniels teaches that placement starts with what a person's pattern of behavior shows you can realistically expect. **You can hope for change without building every decision around it.**

## Section 3: What You'll Learn

### Section headline

## Learn What to Change When the Pattern Hasn't Changed.

### Takeaway 1

**Evaluate: Look at the pattern, including your part in it.**

Recognize where your expectations don't match your experience. Examine what you keep giving, assuming, or leaving unspoken, without turning one difficult moment into a verdict on someone.

**Manage: Choose a next step beyond “try harder.”**

Understand when to reconsider an expectation, adjust what you give, or prepare an honest conversation. The goal is a more intentional response, not another reaction you'll regret.

## Section 2: Recognize the Problem

### Section headline

## Everyone Can Count on You. But You're Running Out of You.

### Body copy

At work, you keep covering the gaps, even when your own responsibilities are piling up.

In a relationship, you're making the plans, starting the conversations, and doing the work to keep things moving.

With family or friends, their needs keep becoming your responsibility. Your needs keep getting postponed.

Dr. Daniels calls this being **upside down in your relationships**:

- **Overinvested:** You're giving more time, energy, or money than you can sustain.  
- **Overcommitted:** You're more committed to the relationship, or to someone's progress, than they are.  
- **Overextended:** What you're giving is costing you the capacity to care for yourself and the rest of your life.

You don't necessarily want these people out of your life. You want a way to relate to them that doesn't keep leaving you empty.

## Section 3: A Different Way Forward

### Section headline

## Giving More Won't Fix What Needs to Be Realigned.

### Body copy

Sometimes the problem is where you've placed someone: you're expecting a level of support, trust, or care the relationship hasn't shown it can provide.

Other times, the person belongs in your life, but the way you're managing that relationship needs to change.

That's why the answer isn't always “try harder” or “cut them off.” It starts with getting clear about **what you expect and what you invest**.

A relationship doesn't have to be an equal exchange every day. But giving shouldn't mean taking on more than you can carry.

**Place: Know what each relationship can realistically carry.**

Explore the five seats: friends, associates, assignments (people you help or guide), advisors (people who guide you), and family. Understand how these roles help you make wiser decisions about trust, access, and investment.

### Takeaway 3

**Manage: Know what to do with that clarity.**

Discover how to approach changes in what you give and expect, when a quiet adjustment may be enough, and when an honest conversation or boundary is needed.
